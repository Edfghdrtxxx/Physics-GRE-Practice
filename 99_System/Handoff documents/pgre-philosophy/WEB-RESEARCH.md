# Physics GRE item design: web research memo

**Purpose.** This memo is source material for a later philosophy document. It collects what ETS, exam writers, prep authors, departments, and test-takers have said publicly about how Physics GRE items are built and what distinguishes them from textbook homework. It extracts principles and tensions, not a catalog of problem types.

**Hard constraints observed here.** No ETS exam stems, choices, or solutions are reproduced. *Conquering the Physics GRE* (Kahn and Anderson) is used only for author strategy commentary, not for book questions. Where a source quotes or restates a released-exam item (for example Conway’s 2009 *Discover* piece, Wittman’s UC Davis bootcamp slides, or Cambridge Coaching’s “3 tricks” post), this memo reports the *strategic claim* and does not recopy the stem.

**Coverage date.** Research conducted August 2026. The user sits the GRE Physics Test on 28 October 2026, so the current form is the computer-delivered ~70-item / 120-minute test in force since September 2023.

## 1. What ETS says the test is for and how it is built

### Stated aim

ETS’s current subject-test content page is the most important official sentence in the literature:

> The aim of the test is to determine the extent of the examinees’ grasp of fundamental principles and their ability to apply these principles in the solution of problems.

Most questions, ETS says, “can be answered on the basis of a mastery of the first 3 years of undergraduate physics.” The *GRE Guide to the Use of Scores* adds a second, slightly different emphasis: “There is increased emphasis on the understanding of fundamental theoretical principles of physics.” Those two claims sit together: the test is not a research-aptitude exam, not a qualifier, and not a comprehensive survey of every elective; it is a speeded, five-option check of whether a typical US undergraduate physics major still holds the core principles and can apply them.

The Physics Test fact sheet, written for departments, frames the institutional purpose more bluntly. The test “can be very useful in distinguishing among candidates whose credentials are otherwise similar.” It “measures undergraduate achievement and provides a common yardstick for comparing the qualifications of students from a variety of colleges and universities with different standards.” ETS cites the Kuncel, Hezlett, and Ones (2001) meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* as evidence that Subject Tests are valid predictors of first-year graduate GPA, cumulative graduate GPA, comprehensive-exam scores, citation counts, and faculty ratings, and that Subject Tests tend to out-predict the General Test’s Verbal/Quantitative measures. That citation is ETS’s own validity claim; independent critiques of GRE use in physics admissions exist and are outside the scope of item-design research, but they are part of why many departments have made the test optional.

### Who writes it

Content and scope of each edition are “specified and reviewed by a distinguished team of undergraduate and graduate faculty.” Committee members are selected with the advice of AAPT and APS. Questions are written by committee members “and by other subject-matter specialists from colleges and universities across the country.” The fact sheet lists recent or current institutions on the Committee of Examiners (Baylor, Beloit, Black Hills State, Brandeis, Carnegie Mellon, Drexel, Morgan State, Temple, Union, Valencia, among others). Membership rotates; John Conway, writing in 2009 after six years on the committee, described an annual Princeton meeting of “a group of six… distinguished professors… working through hundreds of potential and actual Physics GRE problem[s],” completing new forms, adding items to the pool, and reviewing statistics.

Conway’s piece is the only first-person public account from a sitting/recent examiner found in this research. He is “sworn to secrecy about a lot of the details,” but he is explicit about craft:

- Items are short, “to-the-point,” and “just about all of them are short calculations, if any, and take little time once you see what to do.”
- Writing such questions “is a difficult thing to do.” After “about six levels of review,” the committee still finds issues of “clarity, reasoning, and even sometimes basic physics correctness.” The face-to-face meeting with ETS staff “more often than you would think… reveals something previously overlooked.”
- For each new form they “eventually arrive at 100 items that test mastery of a clear physics concept or idea,” with “a certain amount of memorization required in terms of the basic equations learned in undergraduate physics.”
- “There are many problems that can be done using just concepts, and many that can be done with simple dimensional analysis.”
- When numerical answers appear, “the numbers are chosen so as to allow easy arithmetic — no calculators are allowed.”

That last constraint is a design rule, not a testing inconvenience. An item that requires a calculator is, by ETS’s own production standard, a failed item.

Kahn and Anderson, in the resources list of *Conquering the Physics GRE*, report a related production fact: David Griffiths served on the question-writing committee, and “questions are recycled very often (which is why so few exams have been released).” They treat Griffiths’s textbooks as a stylistic ancestor of many items. That is an author anecdote, not an ETS statement; it is consistent with Conway’s description of a pool that is reviewed, reused, and slowly replenished.

Percentages of the test by topic “have been set by the committee of examiners, with input from a nationwide survey of undergraduate physics curricula.” They “reflect the committee’s determination of the relative emphasis placed on each topic in a typical undergraduate program.” Subtopics inside each category are “listed roughly in order of decreasing importance for inclusion in the test.” Nearly all questions relate to the published listing, “however, there may be occasional questions on other topics not explicitly listed here.”

### Current structure (September 2023–present)

| Feature | Current | Pre–September 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery | Computer-delivered (test centers worldwide; at home in most countries) | Paper-based, three administrations a year |
| Items | Approximately 70 five-choice questions | Approximately 100 five-choice questions |
| Time | 2 hours; no separately timed sections | 2 hours 50 minutes (170 minutes); no separately timed sections |
| Mean time per item | ~103 seconds (~1.7 min) | ~102 seconds (~1.7 min) |
| Stimulus types | Diagrams, graphs, experimental data, descriptions of physical situations; some items grouped in sets | Same |
| Units | SI predominantly; a table of constants and a few SI conversion factors is provided | Same |
| Total score | 200–990, 10-point increments, equated across editions | Same scale |
| Subscores | Three *percent-correct* scores (0–100), not equated across editions: Classical Mechanics; Electromagnetism; Quantum Mechanics and Atomic Physics | Equated subscores on a 20–99 scale (Physics tests from April 2021–April 2023); older forms had no subscores |

ETS announced the move to computer delivery and the shortening of Physics and Psychology “so students can take them within 2 hours (rather than nearly 3 hours)” as an access and convenience change, not as a change in what the test measures. The *Guide to the Use of Scores* still describes “approximately 70 questions” covering the same nine content areas at the same percentages. Time per item was conserved; item count and total duration were not.

ETS’s own test-taking advice in the current *Practice Book for the GRE Subject Test in Physics* (form GR1775; copyright 2024) is short and operational:

- Work as rapidly as you can without being careless.
- “All questions are of equal value; do not waste time pondering individual questions you find extremely difficult or unfamiliar.”
- Consider a three-pass: confident items first, then those that need more thought, then the most difficult if time remains.
- Score is the number of questions answered correctly. “Nothing is subtracted from a score if you answer a question incorrectly. Therefore, to maximize your score it is better for you to guess at an answer than not to respond at all.”

That last sentence is a real format change. Older paper forms used formula scoring (correct minus one-fourth of incorrect). Much of the 2008–2017 forum and department advice still assumes the penalty. A synthesizer must date-stamp guessing advice: Conway (2009), Vassar’s departmental page, PrepScholar (2017), and many physicsgre.com threads describe a world in which random guessing is harmful. The current practice book and CPG’s later editions describe a world in which it is not.

### Content specification (percentages and subtopics)

The official percentages have been stable across the 100-item and 70-item eras:

1. Classical Mechanics — 20%. Kinematics, Newton’s laws, work and energy, oscillatory motion, rotation about a fixed axis, systems of particles, central forces and celestial mechanics, 3-D particle dynamics, Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalism, non-inertial frames, elementary fluid dynamics.
2. Electromagnetism — 18%. Electrostatics, currents and DC circuits, magnetic fields in free space, Lorentz force, induction, Maxwell’s equations and applications, electromagnetic waves, AC circuits, fields in matter.
3. Optics and Wave Phenomena — 8%. Wave properties, superposition, interference, diffraction, geometrical optics, polarization, Doppler effect.
4. Thermodynamics and Statistical Mechanics — 10%. Laws of thermodynamics, processes, equations of state, ideal gases, kinetic theory, ensembles, statistical calculation of thermodynamic quantities, thermal expansion and heat transfer.
5. Quantum Mechanics — 13%. Fundamental concepts; Schrödinger solutions (square wells, harmonic oscillators, hydrogenic atoms); spin; angular momentum; wave-function symmetry; elementary perturbation theory.
6. Atomic Physics — 10%. Electrons, Bohr model, energy quantization, atomic structure and spectra, selection rules, black-body radiation, x-rays, atoms in E and B fields.
7. Special Relativity — 6%. Introductory concepts, time dilation, length contraction, simultaneity, energy and momentum, four-vectors and Lorentz transformation, velocity addition.
8. Laboratory Methods — 6%. Data and error analysis, electronics, instrumentation, radiation detection, counting statistics, charged-particle interactions with matter, lasers and optical interferometers, dimensional analysis, fundamental probability and statistics.
9. Specialized Topics — 9%. Nuclear and particle (properties, decay, fission/fusion, reactions, elementary-particle properties); condensed matter (crystal structure, x-ray diffraction, thermal properties, electron theory of metals, semiconductors, superconductors); miscellaneous (astrophysics, mathematical methods, computer applications).

Examinees “should be familiar with certain mathematical methods and their applications in physics”: single and multivariate calculus; rectangular, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates; vector algebra and vector differential operators; Fourier series; PDEs and boundary-value problems; matrices and determinants; functions of complex variables. These “may appear in the test in the context of various content categories as well as occasional questions concerning only mathematics in the specialized topics category.”

QM (13%) plus Atomic (10%) is nearly a quarter of the test; those two plus Mechanics and E&M are the three subscore regions and about 61% of the paper. Laboratory Methods is the only category that names “dimensional analysis” as content rather than as a solver’s trick.

### Scoring, difficulty mix, and what a “good” raw score means

The current practice book’s conversion table for form GR1775 is the only public raw-to-scaled map for the 70-item test. Highlights, without any item text:

- 62–70 correct converts to 990.
- 54 correct converts to 880.
- 40 correct converts to 700.
- 35 correct converts to 640.
- 0 correct converts to 280 (the floor is not 200).

ETS interpretive data for test takers between July 1, 2022 and June 30, 2025 (N = 4,760 Physics examinees) report a Physics mean of 733 (SD 167). Approximate percentiles from Table 2B of the *Guide to the Use of Scores*: 990 is above 96 percent of test takers; 860 is about the 70th percentile; 740 is about the 50th; 700 is about the 43rd. Percent-correct *subscores* (Classical Mechanics mean 64, Electromagnetism 57, QM & Atomic 57, each on 0–100) are **not equated** across editions and must not be compared from one form to another.

The practice-book worksheet also publishes, for a US sample, the percentage of examinees answering each GR1775 item correctly (P+). Those P+ values, which this memo will not attach to item numbers or content, range from the low 20s to the mid-80s. The form is not a wall of equally hard problems. It is a mix: some items almost everyone gets, some items almost no one gets, and a thick middle. That mix is a design choice. It is what lets ETS equate forms and what makes “skip anything that looks hard” a rational first-pass strategy.

Reliability of a representative post-2023 Physics edition is reported as KR-20 ≈ 0.92, SEM of an individual score ≈ 45 scaled points (sample size in one ETS table: 2,745). A 50-point score difference is inside noise.

ETS also tells examinees, in the practice book: “GRE Subject Test questions are designed to measure skills and knowledge gained over a long period of time. Although you might increase your scores to some extent through preparation a few weeks or months before you take the test, last minute cramming is unlikely to be of further help.” And: “no one is expected to be familiar with the content of every question.” Those two sentences are official permission both to study and to skip.

## 2. What experienced writers and solvers say distinguishes a GRE Physics item from a homework item

The recurring claim, from people who have written the test and people who have trained for it, is that a GRE item is a *short recognition-and-application event*, not a derivation. Homework rewards a correct multi-step path with partial credit. The GRE rewards a single letter, in about 100 seconds, with no calculator, no partial credit, and four distractors written by physicists who know which mistakes you will make.

### Stem craft: short, one idea, “once you see what to do”

Conway: items are “short, to-the-point”; they “take little time once you see what to do.” Wittman (UC Davis bootcamp, 2019, “borrowing heavily from Damien Martin”): “GRE questions are typically short—they won’t ask questions that require crazy-detailed calculations!” Robert Brown (Case Western), in a 2023 AIP *SPS Observer* interview: “However we might criticize the GRE, it has beautiful little problems.” Brown’s diagnostic is the inverse of Conway’s production note. He gives students GRE items next to typical free-form problems. They “do amazingly well on the free-form problems but just bomb the GRE questions,” for four reasons he names: physics courses no longer require students to remember anything; many students do not read carefully; free-form work offers partial credit; and students cannot get numbers right without a calculator. “Students right now are as smart as ever… but our field has put them in a situation where memorizing and arithmetic are lost skills.”

That is a claim about *item form*, not about intelligence. A homework problem can be long because the course is teaching a method. A GRE item that is long is, in Conway’s account, usually a writing failure that six review layers try to catch.

Brown also notes a historical texture: “The older tests have much longer problems, so do them without worrying about time.” Kahn and Anderson independently warn that only the 2001, 2008, and 2017 released exams are representative of the *current* content of the test; the 1980s–1990s forms are useful muscle but not a style match. Jaan Altosaar’s widely circulated prep note agrees: “The old tests from the 90s are much harder than the current versions.” A later synthesizer should treat “GRE style” as time-dependent. The committee’s own description of “short, to-the-point” is a description of the form they were writing in the late 2000s, which is closer to 2008/2017/2024 than to 1986.

Kahn and Anderson’s preface is explicit that even *their* chapter-end problems are not GRE clones: they “don’t intend them to exactly replicate GRE questions in style and difficulty: that’s the purpose of the sample exams.” Chapter problems “may feature solutions with more steps than you would see on test day.” That is an author’s confession that GRE-likeness is a separate craft from “physics problem in five choices.”

On physicsgre.com, the operational version of “short” is a first-pass rule. PathIntegrals92 (October 2014 time-management thread): on the first pass, do “problems that require the least amount of calculations… conceptual, anything that can be ticked off using dimensional analysis,” and skip “problems that require a lot of reading… just too many details.” shep23, in the same thread, *disagrees* about skipping every non-conceptual item, but agrees that the items “apparently designed to be ‘longer’ questions: the ones that require a paragraph of reading and a unique situation to set up” are the ones to defer. The disagreement is about which stems are long, not about whether length is a signal.

### “Looks hard / is easy”: recognition, not grind

This is the most repeated test-taker description of GRE-ness.

Physics Forums, “Is the GRE Physics a sensible test?” (2012): “My experience with the physics GRE was that it wasn’t a calculation test so much as the ‘spot the trick’ test. Once you’ve figured out the point of the problem, the arithmetic wasn’t bad, and going through a large amount of calculation was one sign that you’ve got the problem wrong (and that you should skip to the next one).” Another poster in the same thread: “A good portion of the questions aren’t calculations at all, but rather the ability to quickly rule out wrong answers. Things like taking limits, dimensional analysis, etc.” A third: training oneself “to skip questions as soon as I made an error or didn’t know how to move forward” improved the score.

Sanha Cheong’s Stanford teaching page (updated 2024, so it is current-format advice): “You *cannot and should not* do all the detailed calculations; dimensional analysis and other intuitions can almost always eliminate a few answers without any calculation.” Check whether “the expression depend[s] on physical quantities as expected.”

Kahn and Anderson, throughout the mechanics review and Chapter 9, treat algebra as a last resort. On collisions: “Exhaust all limiting cases and dimensional analysis arguments before doing algebra. After this, if you think the algebra is easy, then do it. If you think it will be messy, just skip it and come back later.” On kinematics: “From the point of view of solving problems, however, one should avoid kinematics like the plague. It often results in having to solve quadratic equations, and although this is simple in principle, it is usually a huge waste of time.” On “how fast or how far”: use conservation of energy. On springs: first try limiting cases, dimensional arguments, and symmetry. These are not homework morals. They are claims about what the *item* is designed to reward.

Wittman’s first-pass rule is even more aggressive: “skip anything that looks hard,” because under the old 100-item scoring curve “you only need to nail about 40 questions.” (He points to The GradCafe to fine-tune that number.) Under the 70-item conversion table above, 40 correct is already a 700 — roughly the 43rd percentile, not a high-score strategy. The *principle* (do not spend the test on the items designed to consume it) survives; the raw-count target does not.

Townsend (Wisconsin astronomy, *Studying for the GRE* PDF): “Don’t get distracted by difficult looking questions.” “Identify the questions that you can answer FIRST, and bank those points.” Also: “check questions for unit consistency and order of magnitude. This can save you a lot of time.”

The official P+ spread on GR1775 is consistent with this picture. If some items are answered correctly by ~80% of a US sample and others by ~25%, then “looks hard” is sometimes a property of the *item’s intended difficulty* and sometimes a property of the solver’s misread. Forum culture treats the second case as the GRE’s characteristic trap: a stem that *sounds* like a derivation and is actually a one-line principle, a limiting case, or a units check.

### Distractors: written to catch the almost-right path

Kahn and Anderson, Chapter 9.5, is the most detailed public anatomy of Physics GRE choices. Their claim, quoted because it is strategy commentary rather than an exam stem:

> The Physics GRE is tricky. Compared to other tests of similar subject matter, such as the AP Physics test or the Physics section of the MCAT, the testmakers throw in answer choices that are deliberately designed to mislead you. Being aware of the common patterns of answer choices can help you avoid these traps, and can often suggest the most efficient approach to a problem.

They order the patterns by how much work they save, not by physics topic:

- Choices with *different dimensions*. Often one (or two) choices even have the right units. Scan choices before solving. “The very first thing you should do when you see a tough-looking question is to scan the answer choices to see if they all have the same units.”
- *Order of magnitude.* Nuclear vs atomic vs macroscopic scales; eV vs keV vs MeV. Common-sense bounds eliminate three choices.
- *“Which power of two?”* Numeric choices 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, or symbolic choices that differ by a factor of 2 or a square root. The item is testing a scaling exponent, not constants. “Memorizing the various constants that accompany formulas is mostly useless: all that matters is the dependence on the various parameters.”
- *Same units, different limiting cases.* An angle from 0 to 90°, two masses, two spring constants. Limiting cases eliminate without a full solve.
- *Same units, different numerical factors* (2 vs 1/2 in an argument of cosine, and so on). Dimensional analysis and limits both fail. Their advice: “slow down, work through the problem carefully, and try not to refer to the answer choices at any point during your calculation.”
- *Random numbers* with no obvious relation. Often kinematics. Wrong choices are “likely correct answers to an *intermediate* step.” Do not look at the list until the calculation is finished.

That last pattern is the cleanest statement of how GRE distractors differ from homework wrong answers. Homework wrongness is “you set it up wrong.” GRE wrongness is often “you stopped one step early,” “you used the complementary trig function,” “you inverted the ratio,” “you used diameter as radius,” “you forgot the 2.” Conway’s production note that numbers are chosen for easy arithmetic makes this possible: if the arithmetic is easy, the distractor can be an exact intermediate.

Cambridge Coaching’s “3 tricks for physics standardized tests” (Levy) makes the same point as a method: “The beauty of multiple-choice exams is that they do not require you to actually solve the problem… It’s just a question of learning to identify which choices are outlandish.” Levy’s three techniques are dimensional analysis, (implicitly) physical bounds, and limiting values. Closing sentence: “No matter how knowledgeable you are at physics, you have to learn to treat physics standardized tests as a whole different species of animal.”

On physicsgre.com (thread t=1636, 2008), doom notes a *historical* change inside this pattern: “try dimensional analysis and limits first, if you can do it quickly. Although there’s not as many of these problems as there used to be on the old tests, it will still get you a few answers in no time.” Kahn independently dates pure dimensional-analysis items: “very common on older tests, fell out of favor around 2000, but are possibly making a comeback,” with “5 or so per test, at most” of the fully trivial mismatched-units kind. Tension: units-as-content still appears in the Laboratory Methods specification; units-as-free-points is a declining but not dead item type.

### Figures, graphs, data, and sets

ETS’s stem list is not decorative. Items are “based on such materials as diagrams, graphs, experimental data and descriptions of physical situations,” and “some of which are grouped in sets.” Kahn’s laboratory chapter treats graph reading, log plots, and dimensional analysis of data as first-class GRE skills. CPG Chapter 7 opens with graph reading and dimensional analysis of plots, then error analysis, Poisson processes, AC circuit elements, logic gates, radiation detection, and lasers/interferometers — matching the official Laboratory Methods bullet list almost one-for-one.

A set based on one figure or one data table is a different time object than a standalone item. Forum advice (physicsgre.com time-management thread) is to skip “a paragraph of reading and a unique situation” on the first pass. That is a claim about *stimulus cost*: the GRE will sometimes make you pay a reading tax that is amortized only if you do every item in the set. A homework lab write-up pays that tax once and gets partial credit for the analysis. A GRE set pays it in the same 100-second budget as a one-line Doppler item.

### Time as a design parameter

ETS equalized time per item across the 2023 cut (~1.7 minutes). Conway’s 2009 advice and ETS’s current advice are the same operational sentence: do not get bogged down on a long algebraic calculation, because you will miss items you would have answered in a few seconds. All questions are of equal value.

That equality is the opposite of a qualifier or a homework set, where a hard problem is worth more. It is also why “beautiful little problems” can coexist with “trivia” complaints: a one-line semiconductor-dopant recognition item and a three-line Lagrangian item are worth the same point. Wittman puts a semiconductor-dopant item next to a Doppler-in-wind item in his triage slides precisely to show the mix.

Forum disagreement about first-pass strategy is a disagreement about how to spend a shared budget, not about whether the budget is real:

- Wittman / Conway / ETS: skip or circle anything that will take a long calculation; pick off the easy ones; second pass for the rest.
- PathIntegrals92: first pass = conceptual and dimensional; skip long-reading items.
- shep23: first pass = everything except the obviously long unique setups; 30 seconds of getting nowhere is the skip trigger; “You don’t want to miss easy points at the end of the test because you spent too much time double-checking questions at the beginning.”
- physicsgre.com t=1636: attempt ~1 minute; if nowhere, try dimensional analysis/elimination; then skip-for-good or skip-to-return.

All of these treat *calculation time* as the enemy the item writer can choose to impose, and that the solver should refuse.

### Approximation, limiting cases, expansions, scales

A cluster of techniques is described, independently, by Kahn/Anderson, Wittman, Conway, Cheong, Townsend, Physics Forums, and Cambridge Coaching. They are not “tricks” in the sense of test-prep hacks that work against a poorly written exam. Conway, who wrote items, lists dimensional analysis and conceptual solution as *intended* paths. The techniques:

- **Dimensional analysis** of choices, and of candidate formulas. Laboratory Methods even lists it as content.
- **Limiting cases** (mass, angle, height, coupling, damping → 0 or ∞) both to solve and to check. Kahn: “Checking limiting cases is an extremely powerful strategy if you’re running out of time… eliminating even *one* answer choice gives you a positive expected value” (this last clause was written under formula scoring and is even more true now).
- **Small-parameter expansions**, especially $(1+x)^n \approx 1+nx$. Wittman lists this explicitly.
- **Order-of-magnitude / powers of ten.** Wittman: “know scales of things, at least in your area of expertise.” Kahn’s “top five numbers to memorize” (13.6 eV; 511 keV electron mass; 1.22 in the Rayleigh criterion; Wien’s constant; 2.7 K CMB) are a scale kit, not a formula sheet. Several of those, Kahn notes, are *not* on the ETS table of information.
- **Functional form of choices** (exponentials, sines, cosines; what is dimensionless in an exponent). Wittman: “eliminate options based on dimensional analysis or functional forms.”
- **Work symbolically, plug numbers last.** Kahn: GRE items often contain extraneous information; naming a given 5 cm as $R$ both reduces arithmetic and exposes unused data.

Kahn’s “Derive, don’t memorize” is the other half of this. The ETS formula sheet is “worse than useless: numerical values of constants you’ll never need, a couple random definitions, and three moments of inertia.” The intended knowledge is a small Class-1 core (definitions) plus the ability to reconstruct Class-2 results in a couple of lines, with a short list of Class-3 results that truly must be remembered. Feynman’s “knowledge triangulation” is invoked by name. Moments of inertia, despite the formula sheet, “are *not* worth memorizing” beyond $I=mr^2$ for a point mass plus the parallel-axis theorem.

Brown’s CWRU flashcards take the opposite tactical emphasis — “formulas you need to recall quickly because you don’t have time to derive them” — but the same diagnosis: the test punishes students who expect to derive everything, who reach for a calculator, and who are used to partial credit. Kahn (APS webinar, 2017) was asked about those flashcards and was cautious: if they are cut-up real exams, save the exams for timed practice; if they are background facts, they are useful. The tension between “derive” and “flashcard” is a real one in the literature (see section 4).

### What is *not* tested (or is tested only thinly)

Conway, from inside the committee: “when I have needed to consult a text, it is [the freshman introductory textbook] at least 80% of the time.” “Only a small fraction of the items on the GRE are actually from upper-level topics like stat mech, quantum, and special topics (solid state, nuclear, particle, cosmology, etc.).” “The single biggest mistake students make in studying for the GRE is to focus on too-advanced subjects.”

This conflicts in emphasis, though not necessarily in arithmetic, with ETS’s own percentages: QM 13% + Atomic 10% + Specialized 9% + a slice of upper-level mechanics and E&M is not “a small fraction” of the *blueprint*. It can still be a small fraction of the *items a committee member reaches for a book to write*, if the upper-level items are written from memory of Griffiths-level set pieces (infinite well, harmonic oscillator, hydrogen, dipole radiation) rather than from a graduate text. Kahn’s resource list is closer to Conway than it first appears: freshman text for mechanics and optics; Griffiths for E&M and QM; *not* the later chapters of Griffiths. Physics Forums (2011, “How can I score 900…”): Halliday odd 3-star problems plus Beiser *Modern Physics*, plus limit-testing and dimensional analysis on released exams, moved one solver from 760 to 890. Griffiths *Introduction to Elementary Particles*, Foot *Atomic Physics*, Reif, and the last chapters of Griffiths QM (scattering) are asked about as possible overkill; the thread does not treat them as the core.

What the GRE is widely said *not* to be:

- A qualifier. Cornell Physics Wiki: it “measures if you’ve memorized a lot of facts and formulas,” not intelligence or creativity. Conway: a critic “might point out that it measures the ability to work under pressure, memorization, and quick mathematical reasoning and calculation. Though these are good qualities for a physicist to have, they are by no means the only qualities required for a successful career.” He still argues that it “really does test knowledge about basic physics and the ability to analyze physical situations accurately,” and that he saw a correlation with Ph.D. written prelims.
- A long calculation. REA’s *Best Test Preparation for GRE Physics* is the canonical negative example. physicsgre.com (t=3979): “Most of them do not reflect real test problems (they are too ‘heavy’ and usually require a calculator — remember, you are not allowed to use a calculator on the test!).” Physics Forums (2006): “many of the problems are also set up so that you can only solve them using a calculator”; “completely unrepresentative”; “Avoid anything by REA. The only way to go is to study actual tests.” Townsend: REA “has generally gotten poor reviews… as many of the questions are too involved.” This is the literature’s sharpest statement of what a GRE item is *not*: a calculator-required, multi-page undergraduate problem with GRE branding.
- A complete undergraduate curriculum. ETS: no one is expected to know every question. Cheong: “it is unrealistic to learn all materials that can possibly be on the exam.” Specialized Topics is 9% and internally diverse; nuclear shell models and long decay chains are widely treated as low-return (this is test-taker advice, not an ETS exclusion).
- General relativity, QFT, and most of many-body physics. Special Relativity is 6% and “introductory.” Kahn flags the Schwarzschild-radius mnemonic as “a little too advanced for the Physics GRE.”
- Calculator fluency, computer-algebra fluency, or lab technique as *performance*. Laboratory Methods is tested as *knowledge of* error analysis, counting statistics, detector types, and circuit-element behavior, typically as short items, not as an experiment the examinee runs.
- Partial-credit reasoning. Brown and CWRU flashcards: students must “deal with no partial credit if your answer is not right.”

UVA SPS: “The content of the exam itself isn’t very hard, but doing well requires that you know the content perfectly. They can only ask so many questions about so many topics in so many different ways.” That is a coverage-and-fluency claim: the item space is finite and repetitive (Kahn: questions recycled often; Cornell: “The PGRE likes to reuse questions and question styles”; a Quora answer, unverified here, claims “5–7 exact questions” from previous tests — treat that number as folklore). Finite reuse is also why ETS releases so few forms.

## 3. How departments and academic analyses describe the same object

### Examiners and faculty close to the pool

Conway (2009) remains the primary inside source and is summarized above. His admissions-committee addendum is that Physics GRE correlates with the Ph.D. written prelim more strongly than GRE Verbal does, contrary to “some lore,” and that students “below about the 30% level have a very hard time attaining a Ph.D., though this is by no means absolute.” That is outcome talk, not item-design talk, but it is how an examiner believed the instrument behaved.

Kahn/Anderson’s Griffiths-on-the-committee anecdote is the other production clue: a generation of items may share a textbook’s examples, notation, and sense of which set pieces are canonical (infinite well, harmonic oscillator, coaxial cable, rectangular waveguide, hydrogen fine structure at the level of “know the scaling”).

### Cognitive classification of GRE Physics items (1980s academic study)

An ERIC document, ED318310 (late 1980s; OCR-noisy in the public PDF), reports a faculty-developed cognitive classification of GRE Physics items drawn from an ETS Test Descriptive Booklet (1985). The hierarchy, reconstructed from the readable portions:

1. Correct response from recall of specific concepts, definitions, equations.
2. Recall of a specific equation, principle, or algorithm applied in a straightforward way to a “standard” setting.
3. Analysis of response options (the readable fragment mentions search of options for “correct units”).
4–6. Qualitative or quantitative reasoning applying recalled concepts to a “non-standard” setting (the most complex categories).

The authors compared this profile to ~80 classroom tests from >20 faculty at six institutions. Classroom tests were “almost exclusively designed to tap outcomes in categories 4–6” — novel problem solving — consistent with faculty interviews and with the expectation that “the average test score should be approximately 50 percent to 60 percent.” Categories 1–3 (recall, standard application, units-from-options) were described as more characteristic of the GRE sample they coded than of classroom exams.

This is the closest thing in the academic literature to a *design* analysis of Physics GRE items, and it is ~40 years old. It should not be treated as a description of GR1775. It *does* independently name the three features that later authors keep rediscovering: recall of a compact knowledge base; application to a standard (not novel) setting; and use of the option list (including units) as part of the cognitive task. Homework, in this 1980s faculty sample, was trying to be novel. The GRE, in this 1980s coding, was often not.

### Department coaching pages (convergent advice)

A remarkably similar short list appears on departmental pages that are not selling a book:

- **UC Davis (Wittman):** triage; dimensional analysis and functional forms; expansions; limiting cases; powers of ten; overlearn Halliday and Resnick; Griffiths E&M and QM (well, oscillator, spin, expectation values); Schroeder thermal; CPG; grephysics.net.
- **Wisconsin (Townsend):** four then-available practice exams; bank easy points; units and order of magnitude; 50th percentile is far fewer correct than students fear; REA is too involved; physicsgre.com for discussion.
- **Vassar:** (page still describes the *old* 100-item / 170-minute / formula-scoring test — stale on format, live on strategy) most people do not finish; classify items; each question is 1 point; don’t guess unless you can eliminate at least one choice (pre-2023 rule).
- **CWRU (Brown):** read carefully; arithmetic and estimation without a calculator; no partial credit; flashcards built by “carefully stud[ying] six of the earlier physics GRE exams and the most recent shortened exam.”
- **Williams SPS:** still lists 100 items / 170 minutes (stale); “Many people find they benefit more from reviewing classical mechanics than from teaching themselves particle physics.”
- **UVA SPS:** content isn’t very hard; know it perfectly; take practice exams; CPG is “the generally accepted book.”
- **Stanford teaching page (Cheong, 2024):** current 70/120 format implied by “be fast”; dimensional analysis; conceptual review before burning the few real exams.
- **Harvard SPS / Altosaar:** goal is not to “understand the material” in the course sense but to do the released problems fast; “once you’ve understood a question, check the solutions websites if a comment gives a faster method of solving it by taking limits or physical intuition.”

### Negative space: Hecht and Taylor

The research brief named “Hecht” and “Taylor” as prep-guide authors. This search did **not** find a Physics GRE strategy book by either name.

- **Eugene Hecht** is the author of widely used *Optics* and introductory *Physics* texts. Physics Forums posters ask whether *Hecht Optics* is overkill for the GRE (it usually is treated as optional depth for the 8% optics slice). An AIP *Physics Today* review of Hecht’s intro text praises worked examples that end with a reasonableness check, end-of-chapter problem-solving tips, and warnings about mixed units and the need to sketch — pedagogical habits that overlap GRE strategy but are not commentary on GRE item design.
- **John R. Taylor** is the author of *Classical Mechanics*. Study-plan videos and posts recommend Taylor’s easier (“one-star”) problems as closer to GRE mechanics items than the book’s harder problems, which are too long. Kahn’s own resource list names Thornton & Marion, not Taylor, for advanced mechanics, and the freshman text for the GRE core.

Sterling Test Prep’s GRE Physics review (marketing copy only, not independently reviewed here) claims that “distinguishing between similar answer choices” matters more than memorization. REA is the anti-model, as above. Schaum’s *3,000 Solved Problems in Physics* is often recommended as *volume* practice and just as often criticized as homework-like rather than GRE-like.

## 4. Conflicting claims (do not flatten)

**Freshman text vs upper-division canon.** Conway: 80% freshman book; biggest student mistake is studying too-advanced topics. Kahn: Griffiths is the E&M/QM GRE book, partly because Griffiths wrote items; QM+atomic is ~25% and gets the longest chapter. Both can be true if “freshman” means the *style* (short, numerical, conceptual) and Griffiths means the *upper-division set pieces that still fit that style*. They conflict as study-time allocation advice.

**Derive vs memorize.** Kahn: derive Class-2 from Class-1; keep Class-3 short; the formula sheet is useless. Brown/CWRU: you do not have time to derive; flashcards exist because students forget “even basic formulas.” UVA: understand rather than memorize, then immediately “know the content perfectly.” Altosaar: “Your goal should not necessarily be to understand the material; it should be to ace this test… Memorize the formulas you see popping up again and again.” Cornell Wiki: the test “measures if you’ve memorized a lot of facts and formulas.” These are not the same philosophy. A synthesizer should keep the tension: the items are written so that a derivable result *can* be reconstructed, and they are timed so that reconstruction often loses to recall.

**Calculation test vs trick-spotting test.** Physics Forums split in one thread: one poster experiences “a lot of speedy calculations”; another says it is “spot the trick” and heavy calculation is a sign you have missed the point. Conway says “short calculations, if any.” Brown says students fail the arithmetic without a calculator. All three can describe the same form: arithmetic is easy *after* the recognition step, and deadly if you skip the recognition step.

**Dimensional-analysis items: common or dying?** Kahn: common on older tests, rarer after ~2000, maybe returning, at most ~5 pure units items per test. doom on physicsgre.com: “not as many of these problems as there used to be.” ETS still lists dimensional analysis under Laboratory Methods. Units-as-a-check remain universally recommended even when units-as-the-whole-item decline.

**Guessing.** Pre-2023: formula scoring; Conway, Vassar, PrepScholar, many forum posts: do not random-guess. Post-2023 and CPG later editions and the current ETS practice book: always guess. Department pages that have not been updated will give the wrong advice for October 2026.

**How many items to “nail.”** Wittman (2019, 100-item era): about 40. Townsend: 50th percentile was 26 correct on GR9677 and 38 on GR0177. Current GR1775 table: 40/70 = 700 ≈ 43rd percentile; 54/70 = 880 ≈ 74th. Raw-count folklore does not transfer across the 2023 cut without rescaling.

**Older released exams as style guides.** Kahn: only 2001/2008/2017 represent current content. Brown: older tests have longer problems and are still useful as untimed muscle. Altosaar: 1990s tests are harder; if short on time, prioritize recent. Cheong: do not burn the few real exams as your first diagnostic. These are compatible if “style match” and “skill building” are distinguished; they conflict if a drill agent treats GR8677 as a model of 2026 stem craft.

**CPG chapter problems vs GRE items.** Kahn himself: chapter problems are longer than test day. Brown: CPG questions “sometimes have a little different feel than the GRE questions, but they’re very good.” Forum praise of CPG is about coverage and tricks, not about indistinguishable clones. A drill-writing agent that imitates CPG *chapter* problems will write homework with five choices.

**REA “harder is better.”** Some buyers assumed that heavier problems would over-prepare. Forum consensus is that they over-prepare the wrong skill (calculator algebra) and under-prepare the right one (short recognition). “Harder” is not a synonym for “more GRE-like.”

**What the test measures.** ETS: grasp of fundamental principles and application to problems; common yardstick; predictive validity via Kuncel et al. Conway: also speed, memorization, pressure; still “basic physics” and “analyze physical situations accurately.” Cornell: facts and formulas, not creativity. Physics Forums: nobody is really happy with it; it “rewards the wrong behavior” (skipping). Admissions culture since ~2018 has moved many departments to optional/not-used; that is a *use* conflict, not an *item-design* conflict, but it shapes how loudly faculty still talk about the craft.

**GradCafe vs physicsgre.com as sources of item-feel.** GradCafe’s Physics forum is dominated by admissions, score interpretation, and school selection. Prep-material threads exist (CPG praise, Alex Lang’s page, UW 16-week plan, Halliday). Detailed “what a GRE item feels like” craft talk is thinner there than on physicsgre.com and Physics Forums. Wittman telling students to “see gradcafe.com to fine-tune” the 40-item target is about *score calibration*, not stem craft. This memo treats GradCafe as secondary for design principles.

## 5. GREPhysics.NET (Yosun Chang) as a strategic source

Yosun Chang built GREPhysics.NET in 2005 as “Yosunism GRE Physics Solutions,” originally by typesetting solutions to the then-released forms in a weekend-plus, and later adding a comment layer. The About page is personal history, not a design essay. Chang’s only design-adjacent remark is that some items “turned out to require more work than I had expected for a timed exam allowing for no calculators.”

The site’s strategic value, according to later authors, is not Chang’s own prose. It is the comment culture. Kahn’s preface lists the site as the pre-CPG public solution archive. Altosaar: “IMPORTANT: read the comments of the online solutions, as they frequently give ways of solving problems much [faster]” by limits or physical intuition. Wittman lists it as a resource. This memo does **not** harvest those comments, because they sit next to copyrighted stems. The transferable principle, already echoed everywhere else: a GRE item that has a five-minute solution and a thirty-second solution is designed so that the thirty-second path exists.

## 6. Implications for a later philosophy document (not a taxonomy)

If a drill-writing agent currently emits generic textbook problems, the public literature says the miss is not “wrong topic.” The miss is *form*:

- One clear principle per item, visible once the solver recognizes it.
- Arithmetic that is easy on purpose, or absent.
- Five choices that encode the standard mistakes (inverted ratio, complementary trig function, wrong power, intermediate result, wrong units, wrong limit).
- A path through dimensions, limits, scaling, or a memorized scale that does not require the homework derivation.
- Time budget of ~100 seconds, equal weight with every other item, no partial credit, no calculator.
- Knowledge drawn from the first three undergraduate years, with upper-division content admitted only in its most canonical, compact forms.
- Occasional recognition/trivia items (named effects, dopant valence, order-of-magnitude constants) mixed in at the same point value as a Lagrangian.
- Stimulus that may be a short description, a figure, a graph, or a data set — not a page of setup.
- Not a qualifier problem, not an REA “heavy” problem, not a CPG chapter-end workout, not a Taylor 3-star, not a research vignette.

The literature does not agree on how much of that is “trick” versus “what physicists actually do with a napkin.” Conway and Kahn treat napkin methods as the *intended* solution. Physics Forums’ “spot the trick” phrasing treats them as a test-taking genre. Brown treats the same methods as lost undergraduate skills. A philosophy document can hold all three: the committee writes items that *can* be solved by principle, limit, and scale; the clock and the distractors *punish* the honest derivation; undergraduate instruction *no longer trains* the short path. That three-way tension is the most honest “what a GRE question is” this research found.

## 9. What this memo is not

It is not a philosophy document. It is not an archetype list. It does not endorse writing drills that clone released exams. It does not settle whether the Physics GRE should be required. It is a sourced account of how the people who write, coach, and take the test describe a GRE Physics item, with the disagreements left visible.
